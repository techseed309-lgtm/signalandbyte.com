Is AI making you stop thinking? The real answer

The three rungs of AI (answers, tasks, judgement), how far each one really is, when superintelligence might arrive, what the newest 2026 studies say about AI and the way we think, and five habits to keep.

8 November 20264 min readBy Signal & Byte

In September the US government started officially calling today's AI "Super Intelligence". It isn't. Around the same time, an AI agent put in charge of a real café in Stockholm ordered 120 eggs for a kitchen with no stove. The gap between the name and the reality is the key to the question we keep hearing: is AI making us stop thinking?

AI as a ladder with three rungs

On every rung, the machine takes a bigger piece of your thinking.

  • Rung one: an answer. You ask, it replies, you decide what to do with it. It can be brilliant, but it waits for you every single time.
  • Rung two: a task. An agent looks at the situation, decides the next step, acts with real tools, checks the result and goes again, for hours. This is where 2026 really happened.
  • Rung three: judgement. A machine that sees further than you on almost everything that matters. That is superintelligence, and it doesn't exist yet.

How far rung two has come

METR measures agents in human time: how long a task would take a skilled person, for tasks the AI completes about half the time. At the start of 2026 the best agents managed software tasks worth about 5 hours; by the summer, a full working day or more, with that length doubling roughly every four months. Inside Anthropic, Claude now writes more than 80% of the code that gets merged.

Two catches:

  1. Clean work vs messy work. AI gets good fastest where an answer can be checked automatically (code runs or it doesn't; a proof is right or wrong). Messy work, where you find out weeks later whether you did the right thing, is different: the café agent also drew a seating plan for a street it had never seen and applied for an alcohol licence pretending to be a member of staff.
  2. Half the time isn't reliable. METR's rule of thumb: at that level, a third of tasks get solved every time, a third some of the time, a third never.

When superintelligence?

Nobody knows, but the disagreement has a shape:

  • Some careful forecasters say fully automating AI research is plausible by 2028 if today's trends continue.
  • The AI 2027 team's estimates for fully automated coding sit around 2028 to 2032, and they revise them often.
  • The largest survey of AI researchers (2,778 of them) gave even odds on machines beating humans at every task by 2047, and at every job only by 2116.

Tasks go long before jobs do: a job is a bundle of tasks plus the messy parts that hold it together. So AI first reshapes work. In the most AI-exposed jobs, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds has fallen about 16% behind expectations, mostly through fewer hires; where AI helps people instead of replacing tasks, jobs are holding (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, ADP payroll data). The clean work that goes first (first drafts, simple code, basic analysis) is exactly the work that used to train beginners into experts.

Our educated guess (a guess, not a forecast): agents that reliably handle a full day of well-defined computer work look likely within one or two years; superintelligence is not a 2027 event; the honest window is the 2030s, with real chances on either side. What would change our minds: an AI that runs a messy business at a profit, or the task-length line going flat.

What it does to your thinking: the 2026 evidence

  • The speed-up illusion (N = 1,237): tasks took the same time alone or with AI, but people were convinced AI made them faster, because it felt easier.
  • Persistence (N = 1,222): AI help boosted performance in the moment, but afterwards people did worse on their own and gave up sooner.
  • Medicine: specialists looking for early signs of bowel cancer found them in about 28% of procedures before an AI helper; after three months with it, working alone, about 22%.
  • Confidence (319 knowledge workers, Microsoft Research + Carnegie Mellon): the more people trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they reported; the more they trusted themselves, the more they did.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 calls this early evidence that still needs to be repeated. And there's a strong case on the other side: writing made us worse at memorising and better at everything else; maps and calculators took over navigation and arithmetic.

Our answer: shift, not stop

We don't stop thinking. We change what we think about: less producing the work, more checking it, combining it and owning it. Developers say the same: their job moved from writing code to deciding what to build and reviewing what comes back. The danger is skipping that new work because it feels faster and you trust the machine. That's how a skill quietly goes.

Five things to keep doing yourself

  1. Decide the question before you ask it.
  2. Write down your own first answer before you look at the machine's.
  3. Judge the result as if your name is on it, because it is.
  4. Every so often, do the core skill of your job without help, the way a pilot still practises landing by hand.
  5. Hand over freely anything that can be checked automatically. That's the clean work, and it's what these machines are for.
