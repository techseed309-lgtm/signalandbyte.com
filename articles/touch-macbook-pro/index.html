The touch MacBook Pro: what the $400 actually buys

Apple's first touch-screen Mac has the same M5 Pro chip as the MacBook Pro next to it and costs $400 more. The OLED panel, touch, weight, battery, every price, the Windows rivals, and who should buy which.

4 November 20265 min readBy Signal & Byte

Apple's first touch-screen Mac is not faster than the MacBook Pro sitting next to it, and it costs four hundred dollars more. Here is exactly what that money buys, how it compares with the best Windows laptops this month, and who should buy which. It reaches buyers on 6 November 2026; this review is based on Apple's published specs and prices, independent lab results for the rivals, and launch hands-ons. Prices are US.

The shelf

Apple now sells three 14-inch MacBook Pros at once:

  • MacBook Pro with M6: $1,999.
  • MacBook Pro with M5 Pro (non-touch, mini-LED): $2,499.
  • MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display and Touch (M5 Pro): from $2,899. The 16-inch starts at $3,399.

It isn't a new generation of MacBook Pro. It's a new top shelf above the old one.

The panel

The screen is a tandem OLED: every pixel makes its own light, with two light-emitting layers stacked on top of each other, so it gets bright without driving one layer to its limit. The 14-inch panel is 3024 × 1986 pixels at 254 pixels per inch, refreshing from 10 to 120 times a second.

The non-touch model uses mini-LED: a white backlight split into dimming zones behind an LCD, so a bright object on black can carry a faint glow. On the OLED a black pixel is simply off, which is where the 10,000,000:1 contrast figure comes from.

Read the brightness carefully. 2000 nits is the peak for HDR highlights and outdoor use; held across the whole screen it's 1000 nits sustained. It dims to a single nit for a dark room, covers both P3 and Adobe RGB, and each panel is calibrated at the factory with reference modes for film, photo and print. For HDR video and print photography, this is the strongest reason to buy it. For browsing and documents, most people won't see the difference.

Touch, and its limits

The touch sensor is built into the panel and tracks all ten fingers. macOS treats a tap as a click, so every existing app works on day one, and Apple added gestures: swipe to scroll, pinch to zoom, double-tap a window to fill the screen, flick it to tile it. Small controls enlarge under your finger before you tap.

It genuinely helps in Logic Pro (a keyboard you can play), 3D apps (turn a model with two hands) and for iPhone developers testing touch. But most Mac apps are still designed for keyboard and trackpad first, and for precise work the trackpad is faster.

The honest limits: the redesigned hinge is steadier but still moves a little under a firm poke, reaching up to a vertical screen gets tiring fast, and there is no Apple Pencil support. If you draw for a living, an iPad or a pen laptop is still the better canvas.

The upgrade nobody led with: weight

The 14-inch drops from 3.5 pounds to 3.0, lighter than even the M6 model at 3.4. The 16-inch loses about three quarters of a pound. Part of that is the thinner OLED stack, part a new cooling design that pushes warm air away from the screen; Apple says sustained performance matches the heavier model (independent stress tests come after launch). A Dynamic Island replaces the notch, the speakers have deeper bass, the glowing Apple logo is back, and the ports stay the same: three Thunderbolt 5, HDMI, SD and MagSafe.

Performance and battery

Inside is the M5 Pro, the chip Apple has shipped since March, so in raw speed the touch model and the $2,499 non-touch model are the same machine: 15 CPU cores, a 16-core GPU, 307 GB/s of memory bandwidth, 24 or 48 GB of memory (128 GB with the M5 Max). Apple compared the M6 only with the plain M5, so how it measures against the M5 Pro is unknown until independent tests. The ceilings differ: the M6 tops out at 32 GB and two external displays; the M5 Pro drives three over Thunderbolt 5.

Battery, on Apple's ratings: the 14-inch touch model is rated up to 22 hours of video (14 hours of web) from a 72.6 Wh battery; the M6 model up to 24 hours.

Price the options

The 14-inch with M5 Max starts at $4,499; the 16-inch at $3,399 (M5 Pro) and $4,799 (M5 Max). Matte nano-texture glass is an extra (reported at $150) worth it only next to windows or outdoors. Students and teachers save $150 on the 14-inch and $200 on the 16-inch. Apple Upgrade (a lease) starts at $55.99 a month for 36 months: about $2,016, and you still don't own the laptop.

Measure memory before you pay for more. It's fixed once you order. On the Mac you have now, open Activity Monitor → Memory and watch the Memory Pressure graph while you run your heaviest project. Apple's guide reads it like a traffic light: green, your memory is used well; yellow, you might need more one day; red, you need more now. If it stays green, the base amount is enough.

The Windows side

  • Microsoft Surface Laptop Ultra (Nvidia RTX Spark): from $2,599, a bigger 15-inch touch screen, but mini-LED, not OLED. In lab tests of the first RTX Spark laptops, the M5 Pro wins clearly on processor speed and efficiency while Nvidia's graphics pull well ahead (the M5 Max is faster still). Some older Windows apps run through translation, and 128 GB of memory costs less here than on a Mac.
  • Dell XPS 14: starts well below the MacBook, with Intel's latest chip and a tandem OLED option.
  • ASUS ProArt P16: an OLED panel with RTX Spark.

Windows laptops have had touch and pens for years. What Apple offers is the combination: this panel, this efficiency and this weight, in one machine, running macOS.

Upgrade, wait, or buy?

If you bought an M5 Pro MacBook Pro this year, nothing here is faster; you'd pay for the screen, the weight and touch. On an Intel or M1 Mac, look at the cheaper M6 too: Apple says it builds code up to 2.5× faster than the M1 and lasts up to 4 hours longer.

This is a first-generation design. Touch-ready versions of Office, Lightroom, DaVinci Resolve and Blender are due later this year. Buying from Apple in the US, you have 14 days from delivery to return it: use them on your own projects, not demos.

Who should buy which

  • HDR video, print photos, music on screen, iPhone apps, or carrying it everywhere: the touch model earns its $400, mostly through the screen and the weight.
  • Need the Pro chip, more memory or a third display, but not OLED: the non-touch M5 Pro, same performance for less.
  • Write, code, browse, the occasional video: the M6 at $1,999.
  • Serious graphics or very large memory for less: look hard at the RTX Spark laptops.
  • Mainly drawing: buy something with a pen.
